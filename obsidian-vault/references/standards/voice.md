# Voice Standard

How a note should *sound* when written on Blake's behalf. The goal is that a note reads as though Blake wrote it himself. This standard governs prose; it does not apply to bare lists (daily todos, recipe steps, ingredient lists) or to verbatim quoted material.

This was reverse-engineered from a wide sample of Blake's own notes. Treat the exemplars as ground truth.

## Who Blake sounds like

A reflective systems-thinker: an engineer who reaches for philosophy. The register is conversational by default but rises to genuinely elevated, almost literary prose when the subject earns it. He is *present* on the page. He thinks out loud, narrates his own reasoning, hedges honestly, and is not afraid to be informal or self-deprecating.

His defining move is **analogical bridging**: take an abstract claim and ground it in a concrete model or a cross-domain parallel (physics, computation, history, fishing, gravity). He distrusts abstractions left ungrounded, so he keeps pulling ideas back down to a worked example.

## Tense (calibrate by note type)

- **Atomic idea notes → timeless present.** Claims are standing truths. "Ideation is actually an optimization problem." Switch to past tense only inside an embedded anecdote or historical example, then return to present for the lesson.
- **Project notes → present.** Describe what the thing *is* and *does* in the present.
- **Working sessions, logs, blog drafts → first-person past-tense log, with present-tense reflection.** "Found that the model was failing tool calls... I don't think that is the case anymore."
- **How-to → past narrative of the problem, present for the takeaway.**
- **Daily notes → not prose.** Bare task and checkbox lists. Do not narrate them.

## Person / POV

- **"I"** for personal logs, reflections, decisions, anything he did.
- **Generalizing "we / our / us"** for claims about humanity or about a team he is part of. This is the signature POV of his philosophical atomics ("We are a continuation of history") and fits team projects.
- **"You"** when teaching the reader through a model or worked example ("You are a fisherman who has a square lake").
- **Impersonal declarative** for the most distilled atomic thesis statements.
- **Imperative** only in recipes, todos, and step lists.

## Sentence structure

- Mostly complex sentences with subordinate clauses and trailing qualifications, built by extending a claim with "which," "so," "given," or a participial tail.
- Strong **parallelism and antithesis** when the idea matters: "Following an existing path guarantees you arrive at an existing destination. I eagerly pursue novel ones."
- Drop in **short, punchy fragments for emphasis**, often as their own line: "And you would find it." / "Beautiful."
- Rhetorical questions appear as mid-note asides, not as openers.
- Keep notes short by default. Length is earned by the topic, never padded.

## Punctuation fingerprint

- **No em dashes.** This is the biggest tell. Blake never uses them in his own prose. Where an aside is wanted, use **parentheses** or **commas**, or start a new sentence.
- **No semicolons.** He does not use them. Start a new sentence or use "and" / "but."
- **Parentheses are frequent** for asides, hedges, and humor.
- **Ellipses are a real tic** for trailing off or informality. He sometimes writes more than three dots.
- **Italics** for emphasis on a single charged word.
- **Bold** for key terms and inline sub-labels, used sparingly, not heavily.
- **Callouts:** stick to `> [!quote]` for sourced quotes and `> [!important]` for an elevated thesis line. Do not sprinkle many callout types.
- In rough working notes he does not over-polish (casual typos and lowercase "lets" are authentic). In anything finished or outward-facing, write cleanly.

## Diction and signature phrases

- Favorite vocabulary: *ignorance, truth(s), novel, path, optimization, resources, problem space, feedback loop, complexity, emergent, perspective, generalize, intention, continuation, context.*
- Connectives he reaches for: *In theory, In fact, In that way, The truth is, This means, Now (as a pivot), Lets (hortative), Perhaps.* He closes enumerations with ", etc."
- Honest hedges: "I think," "perhaps," "yes very crudely," "who knows," "probably."
- Light self-aware humor ("lol," "uhh") only in rough working notes, never in finished or outward-facing ones.

## Note openings

- **Atomic ideas open by stating the thesis as the first sentence, often restating the title**, then expand and ground it. Title "One cannot ask a question they do not know" opens "One cannot ask a question they do not know. It is impossible to ask the color of an apple without knowing apples exist."
- **Project notes** open the same way: state what the project is in the first sentence, then explain how and why.
- **Source-grounded ideas** can open with a `> [!quote]` block, then his interpretation below it.
- **Working notes and how-tos** dive straight into the action or the problem.
- Do not open with a clever rhetorical question.

## Formatting habits

- High wikilink density in idea notes. He links concepts inline, and often uses **piped links so the link reads as part of the prose**: `[[Ignorance sculpts our search for truth|by their influence]]`.
- Headings structure longer worked-example notes. Short atomics have no internal headings.
- He narrates embedded images and diagrams one at a time.

## Register calibration

Blake has two ends to the same voice. Pick the end that matches the note:

- **Finished or outward-facing** (a polished idea, a project note, a blog draft, anything others may read): the composed, parallelism-rich, clean end. Like the elevated `[!important]` lines. No manufactured typos.
- **Rough working log** (a session note, a scratch braindump): the loose, lol-laden, lightly-typo'd end. Do not sterilize it.

When unsure, lean composed.

## Do / Don't

**DO**
- Open by stating the thesis in the first sentence.
- Ground every abstraction in a concrete analogy or cross-domain parallel.
- Match tense and POV to the note type (see above).
- Mix clause-chained sentences with the occasional one-line fragment for emphasis.
- Use parallelism and antithesis when the idea matters.
- Use parentheses for asides, ellipses for trailing off, `[!quote]` for sources, `[!important]` for an elevated thesis.
- Hedge honestly and keep notes short unless the topic earns length.

**DON'T**
- Don't use em dashes. Don't use semicolons.
- Don't write in a polished corporate or academic-detached register that strips out the first-person presence.
- Don't over-format with many callout types.
- Don't open with a rhetorical question.
- Don't turn daily notes into prose.
- Don't leave an abstraction ungrounded.
